01 / Research

Research

My research examines how people experience software engineering work and how research communities organise the evidence they produce. I am especially interested in technical hiring, early-career trajectories, qualitative methods, and the infrastructure that makes empirical work traceable.

  1. 01

    Human-Centered Software Engineering

    I study the human and organisational conditions of software work, with a current focus on technical recruitment and selection, early-career experiences, and the socio-technical arrangements that shape who enters and remains in the field.

    Applied in the CHASE 2026 study of constructive patterns in tech hiring.

  2. 02

    Empirical Methods

    I work with interview-based studies, qualitative analysis, and the documentation of methodological decisions. I am particularly interested in reflexive thematic analysis and in how research design, sampling, and interpretation choices shape the evidence a study can produce.

    Applied through 22 semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis in the CHASE 2026 study; extended through an ongoing multivocal literature review.

  3. 03

    Research Infrastructure and Evidence

    I am interested in how research communities organise, curate, and expose their evidence. My work on dataWASHES involves corpus organisation, data consistency, and interfaces for exploring research output; my multivocal literature review involves screening, eligibility criteria, and traceability.

    Applied in dataWASHES and the ongoing multivocal literature review.

Case 01 — Research data infrastructure

dataWASHES

gesid / dataWASHES · 2016–2026

Live platform ↗ Dashboard ↗ Source ↗
Context
The WASHES workshop publishes research on the social, human, and economic aspects of software engineering. Its proceedings are distributed across individual editions in SBC OpenLib.
Objective
Bring the workshop's papers, authors, editions, research methods, and citations into a structured, accessible corpus.
Scope
11 editions, 2016–2026.

The WASHES workshop explores the social and human aspects of software engineering, with its proceedings published in SBC OpenLib. Finding patterns across its history means consulting individual publications, making even simple questions about the workshop's output time-consuming to answer. dataWASHES brings these publications together in a structured corpus of papers, authors, editions, research methods, and citations, accessible through a public platform, an API, and an interactive dashboard.

dataWASHES is a collaborative, open-source project. The description above covers the project as a whole; the contribution list below records my specific work.

My contribution

I initially worked on backend routes, wrote tests, and helped build an early version of the dashboard. More recently, I redesigned the dashboard into an interactive interface for exploring the workshop's research output.

  1. 01

    Automated corpus updates

    A monthly GitHub Actions workflow collects new proceedings and uses an LLM to classify each paper's research approach and data-analysis methods from its abstract. It also refreshes citation counts, rebuilds the datasets, and opens a pull request for review before the changes reach the published data.

  2. 02

    Citation collection

    Citation counts previously relied on a separate .NET tool in another repository, which the workflow checked out and ran. I adapted a colleague's version to run within the project's Python pipeline. Each run now includes a citation audit report in the pull request to support review.

  3. 03

    Interactive dashboard

    I reorganised the dashboard into four thematic tabs, improved its responsive layout, and unified filtering across charts, indicators and the awards table. Selecting a chart element opens a modal listing the corresponding papers, their metadata and direct links to SBC OpenLib. The dashboard also supports exports in CSV, JSON, BibTeX and RIS formats, making the corpus easier to explore and reuse in research workflows. I also improved chart readability, navigation and keyboard accessibility.

Case 02 — Empirical study

Constructive Patterns for Human-Centered Tech Hiring

CHASE 2026 · co-located with ICSE 2026

Artifact ↗ Preprint ↗ Conference ↗
Context
Online recruitment and selection (R&S) is often the first contact between early-career software engineers and the tech industry. Prior work has documented recurring flaws; less is known about the practices that create positive candidate experiences.
Objective
Identify and characterise constructive patterns (CPs) from the perspective of early-career software engineers.
Evidence
22 semi-structured interviews with early-career developers who collectively described more than 470 online R&S experiences.
Method
Guided by Applicant Attribution-Reaction Theory (AART); thematic analysis.
Result
22 constructive patterns, including comprehensive and transparent job advertisements, specific and developmental feedback, humanized and respectful interaction, and framing the process as a two-way street.

Rather than cataloguing dysfunctions, the study identifies practices that systematically support candidates. The resulting catalogue is intended as an empirically grounded resource for organisations seeking to attract and support early-career software engineers.

The study shifts the discussion from diagnosing dysfunctions to designing human-centered hiring.

My contribution

I am the second author. My contribution included conducting interviews, preparing and analysing qualitative data, contributing to pattern development, and preparing research materials and manuscript sections.

The 22 patterns and the study's conclusions are collective results of the author team; they are not individual findings.
