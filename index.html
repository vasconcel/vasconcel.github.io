dataWASHES

gesid / dataWASHES

Live platform ↗ Source ↗ Dashboard ↗

WASHES is a Brazilian workshop on the social and human sides of software. Its papers live in SBC OpenLib, and reviewing the workshop's history means opening them one by one, which is slow and easy to get wrong. dataWASHES is a GESID project that gathers those proceedings into a single corpus and serves them through a public API and a dashboard. Built by around a dozen students over the years, it now covers eleven editions, from 2016 to 2026, with 137 papers and 429 authors.

My contribution

I started out on the backend and API routes, wrote tests, and helped build an early version of the dashboard. Since then I have mostly worked on keeping the platform and its data in good shape. Those later changes were largely my own, checked with my academic advisor.

  • Automated corpus updates. Every month, a GitHub Actions job gathers any new proceedings and asks an LLM to classify each paper's research approach and data-analysis methods from its abstract. It then refreshes citation counts, rebuilds the datasets, and opens a pull request so the changes can be reviewed before they reach the published data.
  • Citation collection. Citation counts used to come from a separate .NET tool in another repository that the workflow checked out and ran. I adapted a colleague's version of it to run inside the project's Python pipeline, and each run now writes an audit report into the pull request for the reviewer.
  • Platform and delivery. The dashboard exports in several formats and searches across the corpus, with every paper in a single table rather than split over pages. Automated tests, CI/CD and the deployment sit behind it. Many hands have shaped the platform over the project's life, including my own recent changes.

Research

My research has focused on people's experiences of recruitment and selection in software engineering. Working with interview data has involved more than collecting and preparing material: it has also meant interpreting participants' accounts, developing codes, comparing observations and bringing them together into constructive patterns and anti-patterns.

This work has shaped my interest in empirical software engineering and qualitative methods. I value the process of moving from individual accounts to a structured interpretation that can be examined, discussed and communicated to others. It has also made me attentive to the practical work behind empirical research, from handling source material to documenting analytical decisions and producing research outputs.

    CHASE 2026 · Rio de Janeiro, 13–14 April
